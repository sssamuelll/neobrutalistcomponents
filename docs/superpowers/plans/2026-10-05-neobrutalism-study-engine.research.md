# Part A — References for themes

## A1. Nakagin Capsule Tower

- **Name:** Nakagin Capsule Tower (Building). Original: 中銀カプセルタワービル (ja), "Nakagin Kapuseru Tawā Biru" (S1).
- **Author:** Kisho Kurokawa (S1, S2, S4).
- **Date:** 1970–72 (S1, S3); completed 1972 (S2, S4, S6, S7, S8). Demolition began 12 Apr 2022 (S1, S4).
- **Place:** Ginza, Chūō-ku, Tokyo, Japan (S1, S2).
- **Kind:** architecture.

Facts
- 140 single-occupancy capsules, prefabricated off-site and attached to two concrete-and-steel cores (S2, S1, S4). One page gives 144 capsules in one paragraph and 140 elsewhere (S7) → use 140.
- Each capsule about 2.5 × 2.5 × 4.0 m with a single round window 1.3 m across at one end (S1); Dezeen gives 2.5 × 4 m pods with a round window (S4); Iconic Houses gives 7.5 × 12.5 × 7 ft and a 4.5 ft Plexiglas porthole (S7) — small disagreement on the window size.
- MoMA calls it the defining built work of Metabolism, the 1960s Japanese movement of buildings that adapt over time (S2); Dezeen: Kurokawa led Metabolism with Kiyonori Kikutake and Fumihiko Maki (S4).
- The concept foresaw replacing/renewing capsules about every 25 years (S6, only source).
- Each unit came with a Sony colour TV and optional audio equipment; materials listed: steel, wood, paint, plastics, cloth, polyurethane, glass, ceramic, electronics (S3).
- Demolished because the concrete and steel structure had decayed (S4); MoMA: after years of deferred maintenance (S2).
- 23 capsules were removed and restored under Kisho Kurokawa Architects & Associates (S6, S1); 14 of them restored to original condition (S3, S5) — not a contradiction, two different counts.
- Capsule A1305 (top floor) acquired by MoMA in 2023, shown in "The Many Lives of the Nakagin Capsule Tower", 10 Jul 2025 – 12 Jul 2026 (S2, S3, S5). Other capsules went to SFMOMA, M+ (Hong Kong) and Japanese institutions (S1); two to SHUTL in Higashi-Ginza and one became a mobile trailer by Yodogawa Steel Works (S6).

Visual traits for a UI theme
- Repetition of identical boxy prefabricated modules stacked at irregular heights; one circular "porthole" per module.
- Colours: in photographs, pale grey/off-white capsules, dark joints, rust-brown rooftop towers (seen in the Commons photos below). No source documents colour values → palette must be sampled.

## A2. Mäusebunker (Berlin-Lichterfelde)

- **Name:** "Mäusebunker" (de, nickname). Built as **Zentrale Tierlaboratorien der Freien Universität Berlin** (de); later **Forschungseinrichtungen für Experimentelle Medizin (FEM) der Charité** (de) (S2, S5).
- **Authors:** Gerd Hänska and Magdalena Hänska (S2, S4, S5, S6, S7); Kurt Schmersow, architect working in the Hänska office, is named among the collaborators (S3); Wikipedia credits Schmersow with the detailed planning/construction phase (S1).
- **Date:** design work from the mid-1960s (preliminary design dated 15 Mar 1967; building permit 3 Nov 1969) (S3); construction started July 1971, with a construction stop through 1976–77 (S3); project completed 8 Feb 1982 and officially opened 27 Feb 1982 (S3).
  - Disagreement on the span: 1971–1981 (S1, S4, S5, S8; Dezeen "completed in 1981", S6) vs 1971–1982 (S2, State Monuments Office; ArchDaily "put into operation in 1982", S7).
- **Place:** Krahmerstraße 6, Berlin-Lichterfelde (Steglitz-Zehlendorf), Germany (S1, S4).
- **Kind:** architecture.

Facts
- Purpose-built for breeding laboratory animals and animal experiments for the FU Berlin (S2, S4, S5).
- Owned by the Charité since 2003 (S5). Use ended in 2019 (S5, S4); the last animals left in July 2020 (S3). Dezeen says it became vacant in 2010 (S6) and ArchDaily that demolition had been planned since 2010 (S7); the project site records that animals and research began moving to Berlin-Buch in 2010 (S3).
- Listed as a historic monument in May 2023 (S1, S4, S5); designboom reports the announcement on 24 May 2023 (S8).
- The listing came out of the "Modellverfahren Mäusebunker". The State Monuments Office says it was launched in 2021 with the Charité and the Senate building department, with four expert workshops (S2); the district office and BauNetz date the cooperation agreement to 2022 (S5, S4). Results were shown 26 May – 18 Sep 2023 in "Suddenly Wonderful" at the Berlinische Galerie (S2).
- Status in Aug–Oct 2025: still closed and unused; the model procedure recommended a concept tender or private investors / a public-civic partnership, and the administration is examining a concept tender; no reuse plan made public (S5, S4).
- Façade: storey-high precast concrete panels 2.60 m wide in three versions — plain, with a cast-in tetrahedral dormer window, or with two outlets for ventilation pipes; the roof edges step back about 7 m (S3). Dezeen summarises it as a pyramidal form with projecting blue pipework and large triangular windows (S6).
- The pipes are painted light blue to signal fresh-air intake for the fully air-conditioned building — the project site rejects the popular "battleship with cannons" reading (S3); ArchDaily still compares it to an armed cruiser with blue ventilation pipes (S7).
- Interior colour code: blue for technical areas, green for mixed-use areas, yellow and red for the clean (hygiene) zones (S3, only source).
- Built on alluvial ground on about 1,300 concrete piles (S3, only source).

Visual traits for a UI theme
- Raw grey board-marked concrete, battered/stepped silhouette, triangular dormer windows, rows of projecting tubes.
- Colours documented in words only: light-blue ("hellblau") pipes; interior zoning blue/green/yellow/red (S3). No hex → palette must be sampled.

## A3. SESC Pompéia (São Paulo)

- **Name:** SESC Pompéia / Sesc Pompeia. Original: "Centro de Lazer Fábrica da Pompéia" (pt) (S3); "SESC Fábrica da Pompéia" (pt) (S2).
- **Authors:** Lina Bo Bardi, with André Vainer and Marcelo Carvalho Ferraz (S2, S3).
- **Date:** 1977–1986 (S1, S3, S4, S7). First stage (conversion of the old factory) inaugurated 1982 (S3, S6); Wikipedia gives 22 Jan 1982 (S1, only source); sports block with the towers inaugurated 1986 (S3); restoration 1977–1982, sports complex 1982–1986 (S6).
- **Place:** Rua Clélia 93, Pompeia, São Paulo, Brazil (S1).
- **Kind:** architecture.

Facts
- Site of a former drum factory: Ferraz writes it was the Mauser brothers' drum factory, later Ibesa-Gelomatic (S3); the ENTAC paper says Mauser & Cia built an oil-drum factory there in the 1930s, later a Gelomatic refrigerator line; closed 1968, auctioned and bought by SESC in 1971 (S6). Disagreement on the factory's age: 1938 (S1), "1930s" (S6; Architectural Record: about 19 sheds of an abandoned 1930s steel-barrel factory, S12), "1920s factory buildings" (S4).
- Bo Bardi kept the factory's early reinforced-concrete structure, which she attributed to the Hennebique system; plaster was stripped and the walls sandblasted to show the brick (S3, S2, S6).
- New sports block (1986), all in exposed concrete: one tower with cave-like holes instead of windows, a second with small square windows scattered irregularly, and a 70 m cylindrical water tower with a lace-like surface that Bo Bardi called a homage to Luis Barragán (S3).
- Sizes: larger prism 30 × 40 m in plan, 45 m high; smaller prism 14 × 16 m, 52 m high, turned 33°; cylinder 8 m wide, 70 m high (S5). The larger prism has 35 cm load-bearing walls cast against horizontal timber boards; its irregular openings (four per side per floor) were formed with styrofoam moulds set into the concrete (S5).
- Walkways between the towers: 8 prestressed-concrete bridges spanning up to 25 m (S3, S7); "four levels" of walkways (S5); four walkways (S6) — disagreement (likely four levels with branching bridges; not confirmed).
- The walkways cross the strip above the canalised Córrego das Águas Pretas stream, where building was not allowed (S3, S5, S2).
- No air conditioning: the openings provide cross-ventilation and carry wooden lattice screens (muxarabis) (S6); SESC describes the openings as "craters" that let wind, sun and rain into the courts (S8).
- Red: ArchDaily notes that handrails, ducts, structures and frames are red against the grey concrete, and that the old sheds were already reddish brick (S9); exposed pipes and iron details are painted red (S10); Wikipedia says the window holes have bright red sliding screens (S11, citing a 2016 Springer book, not opened).
- Listed by IPHAN (federal heritage) in 2015 (S6).

Visual traits for a UI theme
- Board-marked raw concrete; irregular, hand-drawn-looking openings; signal-red frames/screens/handrails; red brick; diagonal concrete bridges.
- Colours: concrete grey + red + brick red (S9, S10), no values → palette must be sampled.

## A4. Craigslist (San Francisco)

- **Name:** craigslist (written in lower case by the company) (S1); Britannica notes the earlier form "Craig's list" (S2).
- **Author:** Craig Newmark (S1, S2, S4, S5).
- **Date:** e-mail list started early 1995 (S1, S2, S4, S5). Website: Newmark wrote software that posted the e-mails to a site that became www.craigslist.org — no year given (S1); Britannica also gives no year (S2); **1996** appears only on Wikipedia (S3). Incorporated 1999 (S2, S3).
- **Place:** San Francisco (Bay Area), USA (S1, S2).
- **Kind:** web.

Facts
- Started in early 1995 as e-mails about San Francisco events; it outgrew a normal mailing list and moved to a majordomo list server, which needed a name — friends suggested "craigslist" instead of "sf-events" (S1).
- By mid-1995 the list had about 240 addresses (S4).
- Posts spread to jobs, things for sale and flats; Newmark wrote software to add e-mailed posts to a website (S1); Britannica says Newmark set up a website with a forum "over time" (S2).
- Late 1997: offered banner ads, Newmark kept the site non-commercial; he worked on it full-time from early 1999 (S1). Incorporated in 1999; Jim Buckmaster became CEO in 2000 and expansion began with Boston; first site outside the US (Vancouver) in 2001 (S2).
- The company's own page lists simplicity and the absence of banner ads among the things users value (S1).
- Wired (2009) described its look as a design from the earliest days of the Web, with page after page of blue links, and said it had rejected most web features popularised in the previous ten years (S5).
- An AIGA scholarly journal named Craigslist as perhaps the best-known "brutalist" website (S6, 2017).

Early browser default link colours (requested)
- NCSA Mosaic 0.13 changed the default link style to **blue with a solid underline for unvisited** links and **dark purple with a dashed underline for visited** links (S7 — the NCSA release notes; S8 dates version 0.13 to 12 Apr 1993).
- Netscape Navigator (Oct 1994) and Internet Explorer 1.0 (Jul 1995) kept blue links on a grey background (S8, only source).
- Today's HTML standard sets the defaults as hex values: unvisited `:link` **#0000EE**, visited `:visited` **#551A8B**, active `:link:active, :visited:active` **#FF0000**, all underlined (S9, §15.3.4 "Phrasing content").
- No opened source gives the *active* link colour of 1990s browsers; the red active colour is documented only for the modern standard (S9).

Visual traits for a UI theme
- Text-first, link-dense lists, default blue (#0000EE) and visited purple (#551A8B) underlined links (S9), almost no imagery, few colours (S5). Live-site colours beyond link defaults: not documented → sample from the site.

## A5. Unité d'habitation, Marseille — and "béton brut" ("classic" theme)

- **Name:** Unité d'habitation de Marseille, also "Cité radieuse" (fr) (S1, S2).
- **Authors:** Le Corbusier, with his studio led by André Wogenscky and the Atelier des Bâtisseurs (ATBAT) run by Vladimir Bodiansky (S1); Wikipedia names Shadrach Woods and George Candilis as designers involved (S3).
- **Date:** commissioned 20 Jul 1945 by reconstruction minister Raoul Dautry; final design adopted Mar 1947; inaugurated 14 Oct 1952 (S1). Ranges given: 1945–1952 (S1), 1946–1952 (S2b), built 1947–1952 (S3).
- **Place:** Marseille, France (S1).
- **Kind:** architecture.

Facts
- 330 dwellings (S1) vs 337 apartments (S2b, S3); 23 apartment types (S1, S3). Storeys: 17 (S2b) vs 12 (S3) — the counts differ because the flats are duplexes; not resolved.
- About 135 m long, 24 m wide, 56 m high; raised on pilotis; proportioned with the Modulor; reinforced-concrete frame cast in place; façades partly left raw and partly painted, notably the loggias (S1).
- "Bottle-rack" system: the flats slot into an independent concrete frame (S1).
- A two-level shopping street on floors 7–8 and a roof terrace with running track, gym, open-air theatre and nursery school (S1).
- Façades listed 1964, classified 1986; part of the UNESCO serial World Heritage listing of Le Corbusier's work (S1).
- At the 1952 handover Le Corbusier told minister Claudius-Petit that he had come to admire the marks the formwork left on raw concrete — board joints, wood grain, knots (S2).
- By using the term "béton brut" Le Corbusier set off the spread of the word "brutalism" (S2); the Fondation defines Brutalism as the post-war current of raw concrete and materials left raw and lists the Unités among his examples (S4); Tate also derives the term from his "béton brut" (S5).
- Wikipedia says raw concrete replaced a planned steel frame because steel was scarce and expensive after the war (S3, only source).

Visual traits for a UI theme
- Board-marked raw concrete, heavy sculpted pilotis, a deep grid of loggias with brise-soleil, accent colours on loggia walls.

## A7. DEC VT100 terminal ("tech" theme)

- **Name:** VT100 video terminal (en).
- **Maker:** Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) (S1, S2, S3).
- **Date:** 1978 (S1, S2); August 1978 (S3).
- **Place:** USA (DEC). City not verified.
- **Kind:** hardware terminal (text-mode interface).

Facts
- Display per DEC's user guide: 12-inch CRT with **P4 phosphor**; 24 lines × 80 characters or 14 × 132 (selectable); 7 × 9 dot-matrix characters with descenders; cursor = blinking block or blinking underline (S4b).

- One of the first terminals to support ANSI escape codes; over six million VT-series terminals were sold, largely thanks to the VT100 (S3).
- Computer History Museum object: model VT100-AA, made 1978, 25 lb (S1).

- Monochrome white-on-black (or black-on-white) text grid, fixed 80-column layout, bitmap dot-matrix glyphs, attributes limited to bold/underline/blink/reverse; beige case with dark bezel (in photos).

# Part B — Context for short essays

## B1. Origins: béton brut, the Smithsons, Banham

- Le Corbusier used "béton brut" (raw concrete); at the Marseille Unité's handover (14 Oct 1952) he said he had come to value the formwork marks on raw concrete (S1). His use of the term is what spread the word "brutalism" (S1, S3). The Fondation lists the Unités d'habitation among his brutalist works (S2).
- Alison and Peter Smithson (aged 21 and 26) won the competition for a secondary school at Hunstanton in 1950; it was completed in 1954 (welded steel frame inspired by Mies van der Rohe, exposed services) and is listed Grade II* (S4).
- Who coined "New Brutalism" — disagreement: the Twentieth Century Society says Alison Smithson coined it, meaning truth to materials and found objects (S4); Tate says the term was coined by critic Reyner Banham for the Smithsons' approach of the 1950s–60s (S3); Wikipedia traces the Swedish "nybrutalism" to Hans Asplund (Villa Göth, 1950) and the first published English use to Alison Smithson's 1953 Soho house text in *Architectural Design* (S7, only source) — the journal's issue is given as November (S7) vs. no. 12, December 1953 (S5, bibliography).
- Reyner Banham, "The New Brutalism", *Architectural Review* vol. 118 no. 708, December 1955, pp. 354–61 (S5); the AR's own archive page for the essay carries the date 9 Dec 1955 (S6, paywalled — only the title/summary were readable).
- Banham's book *The New Brutalism: Ethic or Aesthetic?* — London: Architectural Press, 1966 (S5). The BnF summarises Banham's three criteria as formal legibility, clear exhibition of structure, and valuing materials "as found" (S1).

## B2. Swiss / International Typographic Style

- After the Second World War Swiss graphic design had its "golden age"; the new International Style was also called the International Typographic Style or Swiss Style (S1).
- It combined the theories of the Zurich school with those of the Basel school (S1).
- Traits listed by the Swiss National Library: precision; typographic grid and sans-serif type; simple typography; strong visual impact; rational composition without ornament; photography (mostly black and white) instead of illustration; restrained colour (S1).
- Armin Hofmann, head of the Basel school of arts and crafts, spread the style and built links with Yale (S1); the style was gradually replaced in the 1970s–80s (S1).
- Lars Müller describes *Neue Grafik* as the organ of this "Swiss school", with a rigorous Zurich wing, and as a model for corporate design (S2).

## B3. Web brutalism: brutalistwebsites.com (Pascal Deville)

- Smashing Magazine says Pascal Deville founded the Brutalist Websites directory after coining the term in 2014 (S2) — **2014 confirmed by one opened source only**.
- Deville, co-founder and creative director of the agency Freundliche Grüsse, borrowed the term because he saw web design drifting toward streamlined, neutral interfaces with no brand character, and saw designers trying rough, back-to-basics sites outside a "perfect UX" world (S3).
- The site's own one-line definition: brutalism as a rugged reaction by a younger generation against the lightness, optimism and frivolity of current web design (S1); the AIGA journal *Dialectic* quotes the same line as Deville's definition (S4).
- Deville later split the style into three strands: purists, UX minimalists and "anti-ists"/artists (S2).
- *Dialectic* (2017) adds that brutalist sites borrow the rough look of the early web around 1994–98 and use raw, hand-made HTML (S4). Note: the same paper says the architectural term arose in the 1970s, which contradicts B1's 1950s sources (S4).

## B4. Neobrutalism in UI (2020s)

- Nielsen Norman Group: Hayat Sheikh, "Neobrutalism: Definition and Best Practices", 11 Apr 2025 (updated 18 Mar 2026) (S1).
- NN/g defines neobrutalism (also "neubrutalism") as a visual trend of high contrast, blocky layouts, bold colours, thick borders and "unpolished" elements (S1).
- Compared with web brutalism, NN/g says neobrutalism mixes brutalism with nostalgic 1990s graphics and is more colourful and orderly (S1).
- Typical traits per NN/g: bright primary colours; thick borders and solid lines; hard single-colour drop shadows (its example: black, offset 4 px) instead of soft ones (S1). It warns to keep conventional navigation and accessibility (S1).
- NN/g cites Figma's brand refresh and Gumroad as examples (S1); Envato also names Figma and Gumroad as neo-brutalist sites (S3).
- Gumroad's founder Sahil Lavingia announced the new brand ("new website, fonts, colors") on 26 Nov 2021, Gumroad's tenth year (S2, primary source; it does not use the word neobrutalism).

## B5. Japan: Metabolism and dense web pages

Metabolism
- The movement began with the manifesto "Metabolism: The Proposals for New Urbanism", written for the World Design Conference in Tokyo in 1960 (S1, S2).
- Group: architects Kisho Kurokawa, Kiyonori Kikutake, Fumihiko Maki and Masato Ōtaka, designers Kenji Ekuan and Kiyoshi Awazu, critic Noboru Kawazoe (S1); Kenzo Tange guided the young group (S1); Wikipedia adds that the ideas were first tested by students of Tange's MIT studio and that Tange master-planned Expo '70 (S2).
- Idea: cities and buildings that grow and renew like a living organism's cell metabolism (S1); the manifesto's four essays were Ocean City, Space City, Towards Group Form, Material and Man (S2).
- Few Metabolist buildings were built; the Nakagin Capsule Tower (1972) is the best-known (S1, S2); Expo '70 in Osaka concentrated their work (S2, only source).

Dense Japanese websites (only what sources say)
- MultiLingual: Japanese sites often have loud banners, dense text, several columns and many small images; reasons are cultural and practical (S3).
- Language: Japanese mixes four scripts (hiragana, katakana, kanji, Latin) and can run horizontally or vertically, which looks chaotic to Western eyes (S3).
- Culture: consumers trained on dense printed flyers (chirashi) and wanting more information before buying; brands such as Rakuten, Honda and Starbucks run denser Japanese sites than their global ones (S3).
- GIGAZINE: Yahoo! JAPAN, Rakuten and goo are typical; it reports YouTuber Sabrina Cruz's analysis of 2,671 site images on two measures, density and brightness, where Japanese sites stood out from other countries, and a designer's view that kanji pack much meaning into few characters and that, lacking italics/capitals, designers add decoration for emphasis (S4).

Sources: S1 "[Photos] Metabolism: The Future Envisioned by Architects During Japan's Economic Boom", Nippon.com — https://www.nippon.com/en/images/i00057/photos-metabolism-the-future-envisioned-by-architects-during-japan%E2%80%99s-economic-boom.html · S2 "Metabolism (architecture)", Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism_(architecture) · S3 Douglass McGowan, "The truth about Japanese web design", MultiLingual, Aug/Sep 2018 issue — https://multilingual.com/issues/aug-sep-2018/the-truth-about-japanese-web-design/ · S4 "Why are Japanese websites with lots of text and flashy colors so 'different'?", GIGAZINE, 29 Nov 2022 — https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20221129-peculiar-case-of-japanese-web-design/. ## B6. Germany: HfG Ulm, Otl Aicher's Munich 1972, DIN 1451

HfG Ulm
- The Ulm School of Design (Hochschule für Gestaltung) existed 1953–1968; founders Inge Aicher-Scholl, Otl Aicher and Max Bill (S1).
- Its "Ulm model" was design grounded in science and technology; products include the Ulm stool, the stacking TC 100 tableware and Braun's SK 4 radio-phonograph (S1).
- Gui Bonsiepe, later head of the Cybersyn operations-room design in Chile, was a former member of the Ulm school (S2) — link to B7.

Otl Aicher, Munich 1972
- Palette of seven colours plus white, including silver and an official light blue meant as apolitical; colours chosen by Aicher with reference to the Bavarian Alps (S3).
- Deliberately excluded: red, gold and black (associated with the 1936 Berlin Games) and the black/yellow of the city of Munich (S3).
- Smithsonian names the Games' colours as light shades of blue, green, silver, orange and yellow (S4).
- Typeface Univers was prescribed for a light, un-pathetic typography (S3); Gerhard Joksch drew the first pictogram sketches in April 1968 (S3).
- Pictograms built on a grid with stick figures along vertical and diagonal lines; a Cooper Hewitt poster shows 166 of them (S4); SFMOMA: a complete system spanning signage, print and staff uniforms (S5).

DIN 1451
- Defined by the German standards committee in 1931, confirmed in 1936, under Ludwig Goller (S6); Wikipedia: provisional sheet 1931, official standard 1936, and a 1938 order prescribing it for the Autobahn (S7).
- Used on German traffic signs and postmarks (S6, S7); on number plates until 1994 (S6) vs until January 1995 (S7) — disagreement.
- 1980 revision drawn by Adolf Gropp (S6); the condensed Engschrift goes back to Prussian railway lettering (1905 per S7).

## B7. Latin America

Escola Paulista / Paulista brutalism
- "Escola Paulista" names a São Paulo group led by João Batista Vilanova Artigas (1915–1985), marked by emphasis on construction technique, exposed reinforced concrete and expressed structure (S1, S2).
- Its key works include Artigas's FAU/USP building, the school's main landmark; the FAU/USP curriculum reform dates from 1962 (S1).
- Members listed by Itaú Cultural: Paulo Mendes da Rocha, Marcello Fragelli, Abrahão Sanovicz, João Walter Toscano, Pedro Paulo de Melo Saraiva, Ruy Ohtake and others (S1). **Lina Bo Bardi is not in that list**; Portuguese Wikipedia does list her among the main names (S2) → disagreement on including Bo Bardi.

Brazilian concrete poetry
- In 1952 Augusto de Campos, Haroldo de Campos and Décio Pignatari founded the magazine *Noigandres*, origin of the group of the same name and of the concrete-poetry movement in Brazil; Augusto's colour poems "Poetamenos" (1953) appeared in issue 2 (S3). Second source: see "Additions to B6 and B7" (Wikipedia, "Haroldo de Campos"; it confirms the group and members, not the year).

Project Cybersyn and Gui Bonsiepe
- Chile, 1971–73, under President Salvador Allende: a system to manage the economy in near real time (S4, S5). Stafford Beer was contacted in July 1971 and arrived in Chile on 4 Nov 1971 (S4).
- Four parts: a telex network, statistical software, an economic simulator and an operations room; working prototypes (except the simulator) by January 1973 (S4).
- The operations room was built by Chile's state Industrial Design Group (Grupo de Diseño Industrial) under Gui Bonsiepe, a former member of the Ulm School of Design, and showed Ulm traits; screens and chairs were made of fibreglass (S4). Wikipedia: seven swivel chairs with control buttons (S5).
- Allende visited the room on 30 Dec 1972; after the coup of 11 Sep 1973 the military stopped the project and abandoned or destroyed the work (S4).

Peruvian "chicha" posters
- Posters with curvy lettering in fluorescent colours, printed by screen printing to announce chicha/cumbia concerts, pasted on Lima's walls (S6, S7).
- They arose in the 1980s, a time of terrorism, on avenues such as the Panamericana Norte and the Carretera Central; colours fuchsia, yellow, green and orange; a cheap, artisanal screen-printing process (S7).
- Fortunato Urcuhuaranga, from Huancayo, was one of the precursors; in the 1980s only about five families made these posters (S7).
- His son Elliot Túpac (Elliot Urcuhuaranga) designed his first posters at 12 and turned the style into murals and brand work (S6); he did not invent the genre (S7).

US graphic precursors: Emigre and Ray Gun
- *Emigre* was founded in 1984 by Rudy VanderLans and Zuzana Licko, in step with the arrival of the Macintosh; Emigre Inc. continues as a digital type foundry in Berkeley (S4, S5).
- Published 1984–2005, edited by VanderLans, based in Berkeley and Sacramento; each issue showed new typefaces, many by Licko; SFMOMA credits the pair with being among the first to exploit the Macintosh's freedom in layout and type (S6).
- The final issue, no. 69, appeared in November 2005 (S5, S4).
- *Ray Gun*: alternative-rock magazine first published in 1992 in Santa Monica, California; publisher Marvin Scott Jarrett, art director David Carson; final issue 2000 (S7). Design Is History: Carson art-directed its first three years; the magazine ran about seven years and over 70 issues (S8).
- Its "grunge typography" was often barely readable — e.g. an interview with Bryan Ferry set entirely in the Zapf Dingbats symbol font (S7, only source); Carson's experiments fed the "deconstruction" style (S8).
